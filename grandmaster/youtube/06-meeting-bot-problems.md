## 4. Segments

### Segment 1: The bot is an extra guest

[Title card: "The extra guest"]

A meeting bot joins your call like a person does. It shows up in the list of people, often with a name like "Otter" or "Fireflies Notetaker."

Everyone on the call can see it. And for a lot of people, that changes the meeting.

A client might say less when they know they're being recorded. A sales prospect might ask, "Who is that?" And some hosts just remove the bot from the call.

[On screen: a mock call grid with three people and two notetaker bots.]

To be fair, there's a good side too. A bot that everyone can see is honest. Nobody is surprised that the meeting is being recorded.

### Segment 2: Consent and the law

[Title card: "Consent and the law"]
[On screen, small text for the whole segment: "Not legal advice. Talk to a lawyer about your situation."]

This is the biggest problem, and most people don't know about it.

In the United States, recording laws are different in each state. In most states, one person on the call can agree to the recording. But in some states, like California, Florida, Illinois, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania and Washington, everyone on the call has to agree.

And you often don't know where the other people are.

In August 2025, a class action lawsuit was filed against Otter.ai in federal court in California. It says Otter recorded people without their consent, including people who never used Otter. It also says those recordings were used to train Otter's AI. These are claims, not a ruling. But the whole industry is watching this case.

Most notetakers put the job of getting consent on you, the user. So before you use any notetaker, ask: how does everyone on the call find out they're being recorded?

### Segment 3: Bots that act when you're not there

[Title card: "When you're not there"]

Many bots can join your calendar meetings by themselves. Some can also email the notes to everyone on the invite.

That's handy. But it can go wrong.

In 2024, an engineer named Alex Bilzerian had a Zoom call with a venture capital firm. The firm used Otter. After he left the call, the investors kept talking. Then Otter emailed him the transcript, including the private talk that happened after he left.

[On screen: a headline about the story, with the source.]

So if you use a bot, check two settings: does it join meetings by itself, and who gets the notes?

### Segment 4: Meetings a bot can't join

[Title card: "Meetings a bot can't join"]

A bot needs a meeting link to join. So it only works with the apps it supports, like Zoom, Google Meet and Microsoft Teams.

That leaves out a lot of meetings. A phone call. A call in an app the bot doesn't support. A meeting where the host doesn't let guests in. Or a meeting where your client's company blocks outside bots.

A bot-free notetaker works differently. It records the sound on your own computer. So if the call plays on your computer, it can record it, no matter which app you use.

### Segment 5: What bot-free fixes, and what it doesn't

[Title card: "What bot-free doesn't fix"]

Here's the honest part.

A bot-free notetaker fixes the extra guest. It fixes meetings a bot can't join. And nothing joins your meetings by itself.

But it does not fix consent. In fact, with no bot on the call, nobody sees that you're recording. So the job of telling people is still yours. It might even matter more.

Some bot-free apps help with this. Upshot, for example, can post a line in the meeting chat: "I'm using Upshot to record and transcribe this meeting." But whatever app you use, tell people you're recording.
